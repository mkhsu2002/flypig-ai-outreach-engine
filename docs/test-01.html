Experiment 01 · Japan Outdoor Market

Entering Japan is easy to describe. Finding the right type of partner is the hard part.

A Canadian outdoor-accessories brand wanted to explore Japan. The obvious idea was to find distributors — but the market was full of retailers, manufacturers, importers, wholesalers, and companies that looked relevant for very different reasons.

Outcome44 → 34 → 20 → 12

44 candidate mentions became 34 unique companies, 20 full research records, and 12 final SHORTLIST_READY prospects.

01

The business problem

The brand did not need a long list of outdoor companies. It needed organizations that could realistically help bring an overseas brand into the Japanese market.

Wrong-role risk

Retailer ≠ distributor

A famous retailer may be highly relevant to the category but still be the wrong first partner for market entry.

False confidence

Importer ≠ strong channel

Having an importer role is only the start; distribution reach, category depth, and brand representation still matter.

Outreach risk

Easy to contact ≠ good fit

Contactability was kept separate from commercial fit so a convenient form or email did not inflate priority.

02

What FlyPig actually did

The job was to keep broad discovery open, then progressively narrow the market with evidence.

01

Discover broadly

Use English and Japanese searches to surface importers, distributors, retailers, wholesalers, and related firms.

02

Remove duplicate identities

Merge English/Japanese names, brand pages, directory entries, and repeated search hits into one canonical record.

03

Verify the commercial role

Check official evidence for importing, distribution, wholesale reach, overseas-brand representation, and category fit.

04

Challenge the shortlist

Re-review every qualified company and actively look for reasons it should be downgraded.

03

Where the list changed

The value appeared in the corrections, not just the final count.

Downgrade

Real importer, weak demonstrated reach

The company stayed relevant, but the evidence did not justify first-wave priority.

Downgrade

Strong fit, but too new

The business model looked right, but channel reach had not yet been demonstrated.

Policy separation

Great prospect, wrong public contact route

One company remained commercially qualified even though its public form explicitly prohibited solicitation.

04
